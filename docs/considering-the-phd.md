# Considering the IDS PhD?

Prospective students often write to the department with a version of the same underlying question: *"Is this kind of PhD right for me?"* The official Carlson School pages answer *what the program requires*; this page tries to answer *what the program is* and *how to think about fit*, so that when you do reach out, we can have a more useful conversation.

This page is guidance, not policy. For requirements, deadlines, and funding, the [Official Resources](official-resources.md) page links directly to the pages that govern those details.

## What is a research PhD — and what isn't it?

A PhD in Information and Decision Sciences is a **research apprenticeship**, not primarily an advanced professional credential. Over several years, you work closely with faculty to learn how to identify open research questions, design studies to address them, and communicate the results to a scholarly community. The degree exists to train independent researchers — most graduates go on to research- and teaching-focused faculty positions at universities.

This makes the Information and Decision Sciences PhD different in kind from professional graduate degrees such as an MBA or MSBA, which are designed to deepen a business or managerial career. If your primary goal is to advance more quickly within industry, add a technical or managerial credential to an existing career, or pivot into a new professional role, a professional master's program is very likely a better fit than a research PhD. If your goal is to spend the next several years learning to produce original scholarship — with the understanding that an academic research career, most often in a faculty position, is the typical outcome — the PhD path is worth exploring further.

## Program fit: research orientation, career goals, and preparation

A few questions can help you assess fit before you invest time in an application:

**Research orientation.** Are you drawn to open-ended questions that don't have a known answer yet, and comfortable with the slow, iterative pace of doing original research — including false starts and rejection along the way? Research training rewards curiosity and persistence more than it rewards prior mastery of a fixed body of professional knowledge.

**Career goals.** Would you be satisfied with an outcome centered on research and teaching, most typically at a university? The program is built around that goal; it is not primarily structured to accelerate an industry career, although some graduates do move into research-oriented industry roles.

**Preparation.** Strong quantitative and methodological preparation matters a great deal, because a large part of the coursework and the qualifying milestones involve rigorous methods training. The program draws on multiple reference disciplines — including computer science, economics, psychology, and organizational behavior — so there is no single required undergraduate major. What matters more is demonstrated ability to do rigorous, technical, or empirical work, and a genuine interest in the kinds of questions Information and Decision Sciences faculty study (information technology, decision-making, organizations, and their intersection).

**Full-time commitment.** The Information and Decision Sciences PhD is a full-time, in-residence program, not a part-time or remote course of study. Alongside coursework and research, students typically hold a combined teaching/research assistantship of up to an average of 20 hours per week. Students are expected to be present on campus, including over the summer for funded research. Plan on several years of sustained, full-time commitment to the program — matched by the program's commitment to you: admitted students are guaranteed **five years of full funding** as long as they remain in good academic standing (see [Official Resources](official-resources.md) for current details).

## Nontraditional academic and professional backgrounds

Students enter Information and Decision Sciences PhD programs — and Information Systems PhD programs more broadly — from many academic and professional backgrounds, including computer science, engineering, mathematics, and business, and from prior careers in industry, law, consulting, or technology roles. A nontraditional path does not, by itself, disqualify you from being a strong candidate.

What matters most is not the label on your prior degree or job title, but:

- a genuine and somewhat specific **research interest** — not just an interest in a topic, but curiosity about an open question related to it;
- **quantitative and methodological preparation** sufficient to do rigorous research, or a credible plan to acquire it;
- evidence of **academic potential**, such as strong academic records, research experience, or independent analytical work; and
- a realistic understanding of what an **academic research career** involves, and genuine interest in that path specifically (rather than the PhD as a means to a different end).

If your background is nontraditional, it can help to address this directly and briefly in your application materials — connecting what you've done to the research questions you want to pursue — rather than assuming it will be read as a weakness.

## How to assess your research fit

The Information and Decision Sciences department admits students at the department level, not to work with one specific faculty member — so there is no need to secure an individual faculty member's agreement before applying. That said, thinking concretely about fit with the department's research strengths will make your application stronger and your own decision better informed.

Before reaching out to anyone in the program, we'd encourage you to:

1. Review the [Information and Decision Sciences faculty listing](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/graduate/phd/concentrations/information-decision-sciences/faculty) and read a handful of recent publications from two or three faculty members whose work looks interesting to you.
2. Ask yourself whether you could articulate, in a paragraph, a research question you'd want to pursue and why it matters — not just a general topic area.
3. Consider whether your quantitative and methodological background would let you contribute to that kind of work, or what you would need to add.

This kind of preparation is also exactly what a strong personal statement draws on, so the work is not wasted even before you apply.

## Can the program pre-assess my admission likelihood?

No. We are not able to assess your likelihood of admission from an informal email, a resume summary, or a short biography. Admission decisions are made at the department level — overseen by the Information and Decision Sciences PhD Coordinator together with several department faculty, not by any single faculty member — based on the complete application: academic records, standardized test scores, letters of recommendation, the personal statement, and overall research fit, evaluated against a competitive applicant pool each cycle. None of that can be reliably estimated from an introductory message, and we would rather not offer an impression that turns out to be misleading in either direction.

The most reliable way to gauge your own fit is to work through the [Program fit](#program-fit-research-orientation-career-goals-and-preparation) and [research fit](#how-to-assess-your-research-fit) sections above, and to review the official [Admission Requirements & Deadlines](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/graduate/phd/admissions/requirements-deadlines) page for the current, authoritative list of what the committee considers.

If, after that, you'd like a brief conversation about whether the program is a fit for you, email the Information and Decision Sciences PhD Coordinator at [deliu@umn.edu](mailto:deliu@umn.edu) with your CV and a short paragraph on your research interests and career goals — that's enough for us to have a useful conversation. This is a courtesy, not a required or formal part of the admissions process; every applicant is evaluated through the same complete-application process regardless of whether an earlier conversation took place. For factual questions instead — funding amounts, application logistics, deadlines — you don't need any of this; just ask directly (see [Official Resources](official-resources.md#contact) for the right contact).
